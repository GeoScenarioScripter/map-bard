The bottleneck

A good map story is hard to make.

Authoring an interactive map narrative means being a cartographer and a storyteller at once — and most tools ask you to hand-build every scene, every transition, every legend.

Large models can already write HTML. But they treat the map as wallpaper and the story as a paragraph. Neither knows what the data can actually support.

The wager

Bring your resources. Leave with a story.

Hand MapBard a dataset, some text, maybe a rough script and an intent. It drafts a narrative, checks whether your data can carry it, and asks when something is missing.

You stay in the loop at every gate — picking a direction, adjusting a scene — the way you would in a plan-mode conversation, not a black box.

The spine

Every story is a graph of scenes.

One schema underlies it all: an ordered graph where each scene carries content bound to your data, and discourse bound to your intent.

A scene ontology describes a world; the discourse layer describes the telling. MapBard needs both.

The workshop

Three stages, one contract.

  1. Plan — draft scene graphs as branches; check data–story fit; ask what's missing.
  2. Design — bind each scene to symbology, camera, transitions, and the controls worth exposing.
  3. Build — emit interactive HTML from a template, then check its own screenshots and fix what it sees.
The claim

The contribution is the representation.

The paper's spine isn't "an agent that makes web pages." It's a computable representation for geographic stories — and proof that it makes the agent tell more geographic stories than a plain model would.

It stands on its own logic: borrowed theory is a convergent influence, never a load-bearing axiom.

The factory

A small, self-contained build.

A schema-centered monorepo, TypeScript end to end, with MapLibre + scrollama for the output and an embedded store for everything else — no cluster to run.

And the storymap it produces is a portable static bundle: it outlives the factory and runs anywhere.

The name

Why Bard.

A bard tells the tale of a place. The name keeps the Map— family flag and trades "Mate" for the craft: MapMate is the companion, MapBard the narrator.

Room to grow a line — a maker, a critic — all under one banner.
